1. INTRODUCTION

Interactions between the environment, host traits, and stochastic processes shape parasite communities and patterns of co-infection in the wild (Seabloom et al. 2015). Co-infection with more than one parasite is common in hosts as diverse as plants (O’Keeffe et al. 2021), rodents (Telfer et al. 2010), and shellfish (Malek & Byers 2017). Antagonism and facilitation between co-infecting parasites can modify the outcome of a focal host-parasite interaction by affecting transmission, pathogenicity, virulence, and host mortality rates, with critical implications for population-level disease outcomes (Hellard et al. 2015, Peng et al. 2024). However, new and more detailed scenarios are needed to expand co-infection theory. This study uses a large-scale survey of oyster parasites to investigate interactions between co-infection and the environment.

The parasite community exists within the host environment, which in turn exists within the larger ecosystem. Thus, the importance of co-infection for disease prevalence and intensity must be interpreted in the context of environmental drivers. Features of the biotic environment that alter disease include predators of the target host or of the parasites (Levi et al. 2012, Lopez & Duffy 2021), as well as host traits, such as species (Levi et al. 2012), size (Bolnick et al. 2020), and sex (Bruns et al. 2021). Abiotic environmental drivers include shifts in temperature and salinity across seasons or gradients, among others (Altizer et al. 2006, Burge et al. 2014). These biotic and abiotic conditions may be more or less important than co-infection in shaping disease patterns. For example, octocoral parasite prevalence depended more on water depth and host size than on co-infection (Tracy et al. 2018). Similarly, host species, site, and season were better predictors of amphibian parasites relative to co-infection (Bosch et al. 2020). Stochastic processes can also affect parasite community patterns, especially for rare parasites or small host populations (Seabloom et al. 2015).

Beyond natural biotic and abiotic drivers, anthropogenic environmental change alters the impact of infectious disease on species and ecosystems (Altizer et al. 2013, Burge et al. 2014). Whereas rising global temperature is one culprit behind altered disease prevalence and intensity (Altizer et al. 2013, Burge et al. 2014, Tracy et al. 2019), resource management by humans affects disease in hosts that are fished, harvested, or hunted (Bairagi et al. 2009, Wood et al. 2010). Different practices may lead to increases or decreases in disease prevalence, whether in wild fisheries (Wood et al. 2010) or aquaculture operations (Lafferty & Ben-Horin 2013, Ben-Horin et al. 2018). For example, disease increases with fishing pressure if diseased individuals are discarded back into the water (Kuris & Lafferty 1992), but disease decreases in fished populations if infected individuals are culled (Ben-Horin et al. 2016). Resource use can be essential for understanding the effects of co-infection because opposing effects on different parasites can generate different parasite communities in hosts (Wood et al. 2015, Reis et al. 2021). In contrast to global anthropogenic environmental changes, resource management affects species at local scales, offering different options for reducing damaging disease outbreaks.

Oysters in Chesapeake Bay provide a tractable system to compare the importance of co-infection, environmental variation and management. The eastern oyster, Crassostrea virginica, builds reef habitat and supports a wild fishery and a vibrant aquaculture industry (Hudson 2019, Maryland DNR 2021). Oysters occur across a broad salinity gradient in Chesapeake Bay, from approximately 5 to 27 (Shumway 1996). Studies of previous disease outbreaks in C. virginica have provided a strong knowledge base on Perkinsus marinus, the causative agent of dermo disease. The parasite infects oyster digestive tissue, connective tissue, and circulating hemocytes. It can progress to intense, systemic infections that produce substantial host mortality (Mackin 1951). P. marinus prefers salinities above 12 and oyster infections are exacerbated at higher salinities and temperatures (Ragone & Burreson 1993, Burreson & Ragone Calvo 1996, La Peyre et al. 2006). C. virginica can be co-infected with a second lethal parasite, Haplosporidium nelsoni, which causes MSX disease (Andrews 1966, Haskin et al. 1966). It causes pathology in digestive tubule epithelia and leads to mortality (Burreson 2005). H. nelsoni prefers salinities above 15 and its impacts are also exacerbated at higher temperatures and salinities (Haskin & Ford 1982, Andrews 1983), with a temperature optimum from 5 to 20˚C (Burreson 2005). Environmental forcing of dermo and MSX leads to spatiotemporal patterns in oyster disease across the broad salinity gradient in Chesapeake Bay (Burreson & Andrews 1988, Carnegie & Burreson 2009, Tarnowski et al. 2023). Previous studies of multiple parasite interactions in C. virginica indicate evolutionary change in P. marinus traits and the resulting impacts on oysters following sympatric outbreaks of H. nelsoni, attesting to the importance of co-infection in this system (Carnegie et al. 2021).

Lesser-known parasites include worms in the genus Polydora and sponges in the genus Cliona, both of which are bio-eroders that negatively impact oysters by using their shells as habitat. Polydora worms and Cliona sponges have waterborne transmission, as the larval stages disperse in the water column before settling on oyster shells (Hopkins 1958, Pomponi & Meritt 1985). Polydora spp. worms burrow into the oyster shell and elicit mud blisters as the oyster lays down nacre to encapsulate mud and feces from the burrows (Calvo et al. 1999). In addition to causing oysters to expend resources building blisters, burrows can make oyster shells more brittle and susceptible to predation (Fitridge et al. 2012). Polydora websteri is the most common species in Chesapeake Bay and tolerates salinities from 10 to 30 (Brown 2012), but prefers intermediate salinities (Hanley et al. 2019). Cliona spp. burrowing sponges negatively impact oysters by decreasing oyster body condition and survival (Carroll et al. 2015). Cliona celata occurs at high salinities and builds large burrows, while smaller burrows result from infections with non-celata species (e.g., C. truitti) that prefer lower salinities in the range of 16 to 22 (Wells et al. 1961, Hopkins 1962, Guida 1976, White & Wilson 1996). Unsightly blisters, fragile shells that shatter during shucking, and odors from fouling sponges decrease the commercial value of Cliona-infested oysters (Shinn et al. 2015). A recent study reports that both sponge and worm bio-eroders have lower infection intensity on harvested reefs, alongside reductions in overall reef health and biodiversity (Anchondo et al. 2024). Beyond Chesapeake Bay, bio-eroding shell parasites damage oysters in many shellfish species, constituting threats to aquaculture and restoration (Dunn et al. 2014, Martinelli et al. 2020, Tan et al. 2023).

Despite the physiological costs of Polydora and Cliona shell infections for oysters, surprisingly little is known about the interactions between shell infections and the more lethal tissue parasites. The tissue and shell are very different environments within the host, making direct competition between tissue and shell parasites unlikely. However, their combined impacts may lead to interactions mediated by host immunity, energetic resources, or overall condition. For example, oysters co-infected with P. marinus and Polydora worms have lower body condition than oysters with single infections of either parasite (Wargo and Ford 1993). In contrast, another study showed that environmental factors including host size and salinity drive patterns of P. marinus, Polydora worms, and Cliona sponge, with no effect of co-infection (Hanley et al. 2019). Diseases caused by bacteria, viruses, and protists are the most well-studied infections in oysters because they have caused severe die-offs around the world, but studies on the drivers of these diseases focus solely on environmental conditions and rarely consider co-morbidities, such as shell parasites (King et al. 2019). Therefore, there is little understanding of how tissue microparasites and shell parasites could interact to impact oyster health and exacerbate global oyster declines.

Chesapeake Bay is the largest estuary in North America (11,600 km2), providing a wide range of conditions to explore co-infection in the context of natural environmental drivers. The four parasites occur across a strong salinity gradient, on harvested and unharvested (i.e., marine protected area) reefs (Maryland DNR 2021), and across the different state management regimes of Maryland and Virginia. The metapopulation of oyster reefs could also provide insights into the role of stochasticity in parasite communities by comparing populations at different spatial scales.

In this study, we use subtidal Chesapeake Bay oysters as a model system to assess co-occurrence patterns for three co-infecting oyster parasites amidst strong environmental drivers by leveraging a large-scale field survey of 16 reefs. We explore patterns for P. marinus, Polydora worms, and Cliona sponge, as H. nelsoni infections were absent. The reefs include paired harvested and unharvested sites in 8 tributaries across a broad salinity gradient (~9 to 16 over 200 km) to test several hypotheses about co-infection in mixed natural and anthropogenic ecosystems. First, we test the hypothesis that oyster harvest and salinity explain variation in the prevalence and intensity of each parasite in the oyster population. In addition to harvest status, we test a habitat score based on oyster percent cover and reef height and evaluate the association between harvest status and habitat score, as harvested reefs have lower habitat scores using this method (Tracy et al. 2023) and have lower reef heights when measured by divers (Lenihan & Peterson 2004). We expect higher disease prevalence and intensity on unharvested reefs based on higher numbers of oyster hosts (Wood et al. 2010, Anchondo et al. 2024), increases in Polydora worm impacts at intermediate salinities (Loosanoff & Engle 1943, Wells 1961, Hanley et al. 2019), and increases in P. marinus and Cliona sponge impacts at higher salinities (Wells 1961, Burreson & Ragone Calvo 1996). Second, we test the hypothesis that variation in parasite prevalence and intensity is partially explained by co-infecting parasites when accounting for environmental predictors. Finally, we test the hypothesis that reef-, tributary-, and state-scale spatial factors explain variation in parasite prevalence and intensity to evaluate the role of stochasticity and unmeasured, spatially clustered environmental factors in shaping parasite communities.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1 Sampling design and field collection

The 16 reefs in the study include one harvested reef and one unharvested reef across eight tributaries of Chesapeake Bay (four tributaries in Maryland and four in Virginia) (Figure 1). Overall, 440 oysters were collected from oyster reefs using an oyster dredge, which entails dragging a collecting apparatus along the bottom under motor power. In Maryland, 30 oysters from two reefs each in Harris Creek, the Tred Avon, the Little Choptank and the Patuxent Rivers were collected (20 October to 5 November) and tested for P. marinus and H. nelsoni infection as part of the Maryland Department of Natural Resources (DNR) 2020 Fall Survey (Tarnowski et al. 2023). In Virginia, 25 oysters from the Great Wicomico, Rappahannock, Piankatank and James Rivers were collected (16 September to 3 November) and tested for P. marinus and H. nelsoni infection at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science (VIMS) VIMS/Virginia Marine Resources Commission (VMRC) fall surveys of oyster populations (Southworth and Mann 2023). Oyster shells were dried for diagnoses of Cliona sponge and Polydora worm infections, described below.

We characterized oyster reef habitat in the field from 13 October to 20 December 2020 using an established method based on qualitative scores of underwater photographs (Heggie & Ogburn 2021, Tracy et al. 2023). We used two GoPro cameras attached to a PVC frame and stabilized with dive weights and deployed this frame to record 2-minute videos at 10 pre-selected, evenly spaced latitude-longitude pairs on each reef. The cameras were placed to record lateral views of the reef in a stationary location. A 2-minute video allows for sufficient time for sediment to clear after dropping the frame and ensures that a clear image of the reef location can be selected. Images were then scored for habitat characteristics on a four-point scale with 0 for no oysters or shell, 1 for reefs with less than 50% cover (estimated) of oysters and shell, 2 for reefs with more than 50% cover of oysters and shell but with low reef height, and 3 for reefs with more than 50% cover of oysters and a relative reef height of at least one adult oyster (Figure S1). We assigned average salinity for reefs by extracting values from a GIS raster of 10-year average salinity values for each sampling point (Figure 1, Peters 2023).

2.2 Disease diagnoses and oyster size

 Oysters were diagnosed for infection with P. marinus using the established Ray’s fluid thioglycollate medium (RFTM) method (Ray 1952, Ray 1966, Dungan & Bushek 2015). First, rectum tissue was collected from oysters and incubated in RFTM for 5 to 7 days to enlarge P. marinus cells. Tissue was then macerated, stained with Lugol’s iodine (Perkinsus cells stain black), and examined using light microscopy. Infection intensity was evaluated by each state’s shellfish pathology laboratory, using the standard methods of that state. When P. marinus was detected, infection intensities were rated rare to heavy in intensity on an eight-point scale in Maryland or a five-point scale in Virginia (Carnegie & Burreson 2009, Dungan & Bushek 2015). Infections scored using the Maryland scale were translated to the Virginia scale using a conversion table (Dungan & ODRP Staff 2018). The resulting scores are 0 (no Perkinsus), 0.5 (rare), 1 (light), 3 (medium), and 5 (heavy). Shells were labeled to link RFTM diagnoses with shell infections for each oyster. Infection with H. nelsoni was diagnosed using standard paraffin histopathology, with six-micron paraffin sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin and similarly evaluated using light microscopy (Carnegie and Burreson 2011). Intensity was not rated because no H. nelsoni infections were detected.

 Infection with Cliona sponges was determined by the presence of characteristic pinhole-sized burrows on the outside of oyster valves (Figure 2A). Intensity was assessed visually by estimating the percent cover of burrows on the outside of both oyster valves in 10% increments. Polydora infection was determined by the presence of mud blisters inside both oyster valves, with intensity quantified as the percent area of blisters on both inner shells in Image J (Figure 2B) (Schindelin et al. 2012, Morse et al. 2015). Blister area is a reliable metric of the damage to the oyster host and previous work showed a strong positive correlation with the number of worms infesting an oyster (Davinack et al. 2024).

We tested two metrics of oyster size to include in the models as covariates. First, we measured shell height as the longest extent from umbo to bill on each oyster. Second, we calculated the shell area of both valves of each oyster in ImageJ.

2.3 Statistical analyses

 For each pair of parasites, we first tested co-occurrence patterns across all 440 oysters and 16 reef sites using Pearson’s Chi-squared test in the MASS package in R (Venables & Ripley 2002, R Core Team, 2021). This simple analysis only uses parasite prevalence data (not intensity) and indicates the overall correlation between pairs of parasites. However, it only captures co-occurrence for multiple parasites and does not compare the relative importance of environmental conditions, co-infection, host traits, or spatial patterns.

We then used multivariate linear models (LMs) and generalized linear models (GLMs) to examine multiple predictors of each parasite’s prevalence and intensity. LMs and GLMs have several advantages, including flexibility with non-parametric distributions that are useful for parasite prevalence (presence/absence data) and intensity (often overdispersed counts or ordinal categories). Applying LMs and GLMs for the statistical analyses allowed us to attribute patterns in parasite prevalence and intensity to certain predictors, compare multiple models in a single framework, and assess the relative importance of different predictors.

Predictors tested in the multivariate models include salinity (VIMS 10-year average), reef harvest status (yes or no), the median reef habitat score from the 10 GoPro sampling sites (0 to 3, Figure S1), oyster shell height (in mm), shell area (square mm), the intensity of Polydora blisters (for Cliona and P. marinus models), the presence and intensity of Cliona burrows (for Polydora and P. marinus models), and the presence of P. marinus infection (for Polydora and Cliona models). For each set of models, numeric predictors (shell height, shell area, and salinity) were rescaled using R package arm (Gelman & Su 2022) and tested for interference using correlation matrices. For categorical predictors, interference was tested by comparing estimates in single and paired models. We tested all additive models, unless interference was detected, based on inflated or reduced model estimates. The additive models include both co-infecting parasites to test the effects of co-infection with 2 and 3 parasites. We also tested interactive effects that were ecologically plausible and statistically feasible (see Supplementary Tables in the Appendix).

LMs were used for Polydora mud blister intensity under a parametric distribution. We did not assess predictors of Polydora prevalence because it approached 100%. Given the binary response (present or absent), logistic regressions were carried out using GLMs with a binomial distribution for P. marinus and Cliona prevalence. We used different GLMs to analyze P. marinus and Cliona infection intensities, which were conducted only for infected oysters, while omitting oysters with intensities of 0. For P. marinus, we analyzed predictors of infection intensity in the 229 oysters (from 15 reefs) that had dermo disease (Table S1). We used ordinal logistic regression (GLM in R package MASS, Venables & Ripley 2002) because this is the most appropriate approach for a response variable with four ordered categories (0s omitted): P. marinus infection intensities of 0.5 (rare), 1 (light), 3 (moderate), and 5 (heavy). For Cliona intensity, we analyzed predictors of infection intensity in the 232 oysters (14 reefs) with Cliona shell infestations (Table S1). We scored infections in 10% increments from 10 to 100%. To interpret the results more easily, for the analysis, these 10 categories were simplified into two categories: less than 50% cover (low intensity) and greater than or equal to 50% cover (high intensity). With this binary response, we used a logistic regression (GLM with a binomial distribution) to assess predictors of high intensity.

The habitat scores could only be analyzed in a subset of the data because the GoPro camera rig was not deployed on one reef in the Piankatank. To address these missing data, we first assessed the best model for each parasite’s prevalence in all 16 reefs without habitat score as a predictor. These results are reported as the “full dataset” hereafter and in the figure captions. We then assessed models including habitat score for a subset of the data, excluding Piankatank reefs (14 reefs, N=390). These results including habitat are reported as the “habitat subset” hereafter and in the figure captions. The habitat subset was further reduced into a subset for P. marinus intensity (N=192 across 13 reefs) and a subset for Cliona intensity (N=182 across 12 reefs), including oysters that were infected with P. marinus and Cliona, respectively (i.e. no intensities of 0) (Table S1). If habitat score was included in the best model for the habitat subset, the results are reported for both the full dataset and the habitat subset. In addition to the LMs and GLMs that include habitat score as a predictor, we also tested the concordance of habitat scores and harvest status using Pearson’s Chi-squared test in the MASS package in R (Venables & Ripley 2002, R Core Team, 2021).

In addition to testing the environmental, co-infection, host trait (size and habitat score) predictors above, we used the same LMs and GLMs to test spatial predictors. Thus, we tested a set of multivariate models with specific predictors (e.g., shell height, salinity, co-infection) as well as a set of spatial models as predictors for all five parasite metrics: P. marinus prevalence and intensity, Cliona prevalence and intensity, and Polydora blister intensity. We tested models with a single spatial predictor at three spatial scales: reef, tributary, and state.

The model selection step was used to compare multivariate and spatial models and report the best of each set of models. First, we identified the best multivariate model with specific, known parameters from the environmental, co-infection, and host trait predictors. We then added the spatial models to the multivariate models to determine whether reef, tributary or state was the best spatial predictor, and whether spatial models performed better than the multivariate models. We conducted step-wise model selection using drop1 and compared models using Akaike’s Information Criterion (ΔAIC). When multiple models fell within two AIC units, AIC weights are reported to reflect the contribution of each model. Collinearity of predictors in the best model was tested using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) in the car package in R (Fox & Weisberg, 2019). Significant predictors in the best model were determined using a significance level of alpha= 0.05.

Model diagnostics for linear models included plotting the best model to assess fitted vs. residual values, outliers, and the fit to the normal distribution. We assessed the explanatory power of linear models using Adjusted R2 values. Model diagnostics for logistic regressions included using a training data set to assess accuracy and plotting standardized residuals of outliers to ensure that they did not have high influence. For the ordinal logistic regression of P. marinus intensity, we used the brant test to assess the assumption of parallel odds (R package brant, Schlegel & Steenbergen 2020). We report estimates and confidence intervals (CI) for all models, and odds ratios (OR) for logistic regressions. Relative importance (RI) is reported for predictors in multivariate models using R package caret (Kuhn 2008), except for ordinal logistic regressions where it is not supported. The results are plotted using R packages effects (Fox 2003) and ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), with significant predictors plotted separately, unless the best model included a significant interaction. The results of logistic regression and ordinal logistic regression are plotted with probability on the y-axis, indicating the probability of infection for prevalence models and the probability of the high-intensity infection for intensity models.

3. RESULTS

Overall, co-infecting parasites explained some variation in most of the parasite metrics. However, environmental conditions were stronger predictors and typically explained parasite metrics in combination with co-infections. Habitat scores were higher on unharvested reefs (Chi-squared = 171.7, df = 2, p < 2.20e-16, Table S2).

3.1 Simple parasite co-occurrence and overall prevalence

P. marinus infections had a prevalence of 52.0% across all 440 oysters from 16 reefs, while Cliona sponge infections had a prevalence of 52.7% and Polydora blisters had a prevalence of 98.6%. P. marinus and Cliona co-infected 33.9% of the 440 oysters and positively co-occurred in oyster hosts (Chi-squared = 28.1, df = 1, p = 1.1 e-07). Co-occurrence tests of Polydora with Cliona and P. marinus were not conducted because Polydora blisters were present in almost every oyster. Of the 440 oysters, 51.8% were co-infected with Polydora and Cliona, 51.4% were co-infected with Polydora and P. marinus, and 33.2% were co-infected with all three parasites (Figure 3).

3.2 Linear and Generalized Linear Models

3.2.1 P. marinus prevalence and intensity

 P. marinus infection prevalence increased with increasing salinity and in co-infections with Cliona sponge. The best multivariate model of P. marinus infection prevalence included a significant interaction between salinity and co-infection with Cliona sponge. This model was a better fit than a model with only salinity, a model with only Cliona, or a model with both (Tables S3, S4). Higher salinities increased the odds of oysters being infected with P. marinus, a 154% increase with every unit increase in salinity (OR= 2.54, CI= 1.54 - 4.18). The odds of P. marinus infection increased by 80% in oysters co-infected with Cliona sponge, relative to those not infected with Cliona (OR= 1.80, CI= 1.14 - 2.85). For the interaction, co-infection with Cliona corresponded to a 68% lower effect of salinity on the odds of P. marinus infection, though salinity still positively predicted P. marinus prevalence (OR= 0.32, CI= 0.117 - 0.861). In other words, P. marinus infection increased with salinity in oysters co-infected with Cliona, but the slope of the salinity relationship was shallower than in oysters without Cliona co-infection. (Figure 4A). Salinity had the highest relative importance (RI= 3.7) compared to Cliona co-infection (2.5) and the interaction (2.3).

In the habitat subset, P. marinus infection was lowest on reefs with mid-range habitat scores. Specifically, the odds of P. marinus infection were lower on reefs that had high oyster percent cover and low reef height (mid-range habitat characteristics, score of 2) relative to reefs with low percent cover (score of 1) and reefs with high cover and reef height (score of 3) (Tables S5, S6, Figure 4B). However, salinity still had the highest relative importance (RI= 5.5) in the habitat subset compared to the importance of habitat scores of 2 (3.6), scores of 3 (0.083), and Polydora co-infection (2.3). Spatial patterns explained additional variation beyond the environmental and host predictors, as reef was the best model overall for the full dataset and habitat subset (Table S7-S10, Figure 4C).

The best model of P. marinus intensity showed lower infection intensity in oysters co-infected with Cliona sponge. The odds of oysters having higher infection intensity scores decreased 45% with Cliona co-infection (OR= 0.55, CI = 0.31-0.98) (Figure 5A, Tables S11, S12). Salinity was included in the best model, but the decrease in P. marinus intensity at higher salinities was not significant (Figure 5B). The best model of P. marinus intensity has an AIC weight of 0.37 and contributing models within 2 AIC include a model with Cliona alone (AIC weight of 0.2) and a model with an increase in infection intensity with Polydora mud blister intensity (AIC weight of 0.18) (Tables S13, S14). Spatial patterns at the state level also contributed to variation, with higher intensities being less likely in Virginia (Table S15, S16). Habitat score did not explain patterns of P. marinus intensity.

3.2.2 Polydora worm intensity

Mud blister intensity from Polydora worm infections increased in association with co-infections with P. marinus (estimate= 0.784, CI 0.208, 1.36, RI= 2.7) and decreased with increasing salinities (estimate= -1.42, CI -2.23, -0.62, RI= 5.8) (Adjusted R2= 0.07, Figure 6A, Tables S17-S18). In the habitat subset, the best model showed a decrease in mud blister intensity in concordance with higher habitat scores (scores 2 and 3) that denote greater oyster percent cover and reef height (score 2 RI= 3.1, score 3 RI= 3.2), as well as an increase with P. marinus co-infection (RI= 2.2) and a decrease with salinity (RI= 4.1) (Figure 6B, Tables S19-S20). Spatial patterns explained additional variation beyond the environmental and host predictors, as reef was the best model overall for the full dataset (Adjusted R2 = 0.12) and the habitat subset (Adjusted R2 = 0.25) (Table S21-S22, Figure 6C).

3.2.2 Cliona sponge prevalence and intensity

 Cliona sponge prevalence was best explained by P. marinus, salinity, and oyster shell height. The odds of oysters being infected with Cliona sponge increased 107% with P. marinus co-infection (OR= 2.07, CI 1.32-3.28, RI= 3.14) and 1,350% with every unit increase in salinity (OR = 14.5, CI 8.01-27.6, RI= 8.50). The odds of Cliona sponge infection decreased 54% for every unit increase in shell height (OR= 0.46, CI 0.279-0.744, RI= 3.10) (Figure 7, Tables S23-24). Habitat score did not explain patterns of Cliona prevalence. Spatial patterns explained additional variation beyond the environmental and host predictors, as reef is the best model overall for Cliona prevalence (Tables S25-S26).

High-intensity Cliona infections (covering more than 50% of the external oyster shell) increased in concordance with higher salinities and on unharvested reefs. The odds of high-intensity Cliona infections increased by 48% for each unit increase in salinity (OR= 1.48, CI 1.20-1.84, RI= 3.6). The odds of high-intensity infections decreased by 73% on harvested reefs relative to unharvested reefs (OR = 0.27, CI: 0.13-0.54, RI = 3.6) (Figure 8, Tables S27-S28). Habitat score did not explain patterns of Cliona intensity. Spatial patterns explained additional variation beyond the environmental and host predictors, as reef was the best model overall (Figure 8, Tables S29- S30).

4. DISCUSSION

 Co-infecting parasites respond to different conditions as they grow and reproduce at the expense of hosts, including both biotic and abiotic environment, host traits, and stochastic processes (Seabloom et al. 2015, Bolnick et al. 2020). Unlike single infections, parasites in co-infections may also affect each other, yet the relative importance of these relationships is not clear. This study uses paired harvested and unharvested oyster reefs across Chesapeake Bay to show that salinity, oyster harvest, and co-infection explained variation in parasite prevalence and intensity. Salinity was of greater importance for most parasite metrics. Host traits and habitat characteristics only explained variation in some parasite metrics. Finally, spatial variation at the smallest of the three spatial scales (reef-level) explained additional variation in most parasite metrics (Figure 9).

Co-infecting parasites were significantly linked with the infection patterns for each of the three parasites detected in the survey. Although infections with all three parasites were common (33%), multiple co-infecting parasites were not included in the best model for any of the parasite metrics. P. marinus mediated statistical relationships between all three parasites. First, P. marinus prevalence increased in co-infections with Cliona sponge. P. marinus infection in turn predicted higher prevalence of Cliona sponge as well as higher intensities of Polydora worm mud blisters (Figure 9). P. marinus and Cliona sponge also positively co-occurred across the bay-wide sample sites in the simple co-occurrence analyses, though the statistical models indicate that this was due primarily to shared salinity requirements of the two parasites. Co-infection was not a strong predictor of parasite prevalence, as indicated by its lower importance relative to salinity and the superior performance of spatial models. Despite its minor importance, the central role for P. marinus is interesting given that it has been monitored and studied in Chesapeake Bay for over 70 years (Andrews & Hewatt 1957, Burreson & Ragone Calvo 1996). Interactions between P. marinus and shell bio-eroding parasites could account for some of the known spatial and interannual variation in dermo disease. Dermo prevalence ranges from 0% to more than 60% across sites each year in the annual Maryland surveys, with variation between sites in similar salinity zones (Tarnowski et al. 2023). Further understanding of dermo variability across space and time would be valuable, as this disease affects restoration, aquaculture, and the wild fishery in Chesapeake Bay.

While field surveys captured patterns in oyster parasites, the statistical relationships do not test causality. The mechanisms behind the positive parasite associations are therefore outside the scope of this study, but theoretically include shared habitat requirements (i.e., similar environmental niches leading to positive co-occurrence) and within-host interactions mediated by immunity or resources that amplify transmission (Pomponi & Meritt 1985, Hellard et al. 2015). Salinity habitat requirements are measured and accounted for in the models herein through long-term salinity averages, yet co-infection is still important, indicating a role for additional unmeasured habitat variables or within-host interactions. In addition to the possible role of shared habitat requirements or within-host interactions, parasite co-occurrence can also be driven by stochastic processes (Seabloom et al. 2015). Laboratory studies are needed to further understand this relationship, as patterns of co-occurrence in a field survey do not reliably indicate the nature of a parasite interaction (Johnson and Buller 2011).

Taken together, the survey results support our hypotheses that co-infection is less important relative to environmental conditions. However, the environmental predictors differed in the strength of their associations with oyster parasites. Only salinity was typically more important than co-infection. The associations with salinity are largely consistent with prior research because P. marinus prevalence and Cliona prevalence and intensity increase with salinity, while Polydora worm mud blister intensities peak at the lower end around 9 (Wells 1961, Hopkins 1962, Burreson & Ragone Calvo 1996).

P. marinus infection intensity was the only parasite metric for which co-infection (by Cliona sponge) was more important than salinity. The negative or absent association of P. marinus intensity with salinity in this study was unexpected based on previous research that showed increasing intensity of dermo at higher salinities (Ragone & Burreson 1993, La Peyre et al 2006). High salinity Virginia oysters were sampled earlier in the fall so timing differences could account for this pattern if it was too early to capture peak intensities, but the dermo season begins earlier in Virginia as well, so this is less likely. It is also possible that salinity is a proxy for other conditions that decrease P. marinus intensity, as biodiversity changes across the salinity range (~5 to 27) that oysters inhabit in Chesapeake Bay and includes major shifts in organisms of all kinds, from bacterioplankton to fish (Shumway 1996, Buchheister et al. 2013, Wang et al. 2020). In addition to the unexpectedly low importance of salinity for P. marinus intensity, it is difficult to hypothesize mechanisms that link Cliona sponge co-infections with both greater P. marinus prevalence and lower P. marinus intensity. Cliona co-infection could generate these patterns if co-infection with the sponge suppresses oyster immunity and facilitates P. marinus infection, but competition between the two parasites leads to lower P. marinus intensity once infection occurs. Another potential explanation is that high-salinity oysters are developing resistance or tolerance to the parasite (Carnegie & Burreson 2009, Carnegie 2021), which could manifest as lower infection intensities. Salinity and disease tolerance could be decoupling patterns in dermo prevalence and intensity if higher salinities increase the chance of P. marinus infection (i.e., increase prevalence), while the evolution of disease resistance in oysters at high-salinity sites acts to decrease intensities at those sites.

Oyster shell height, or size, was only a significant predictor of Cliona infection prevalence, with larger oysters having lower odds of Cliona infection. This finding was contrary to our expectations based on previous studies finding increases in disease risk in older and larger organisms (Lafferty & Harvell 2014, Groner et al. 2014, Yoshioka et al. 2016, Tracy et al. 2018, Behringer et al. 2020, Tan et al. 2023). Additionally, oyster shell height predicted Cliona prevalence, but not intensity. Increased disease prevalence in smaller oysters could result from oysters diverting energy to growth in early life stages. There could also be a cohort effect if older oysters experienced early-life conditions that made them less susceptible to Cliona infections, such as the extreme low salinities in Chesapeake Bay in 2018 and 2019 (Tarnowski et al. 2023). Likewise, smaller and younger oysters could have experienced stressful conditions that increased infection rates at vulnerable juvenile stages. The age-based susceptibility of C. virginica oysters to bio-eroding organisms is not clear. A study in the Gulf of Mexico found higher intensity in smaller oysters but no effect on prevalence (Hanley et al. 2019), while a study in North Carolina detected higher sponge biomass in larger oysters (Carroll et al. 2015). Although shell height was not included among the best predictors of Polydora mud blister area in this study, previous studies have reported higher numbers of Polydora mud blisters in older, larger oysters (Clements et al. 2017, de Souza et al. 2017), though noting the conflation of age, size, and cohort (Loosanoff & Engle 1943). Further research is needed to separate age, size, and cohort effects, perhaps using experimental outplants with hatchery-spawned oysters of known ages.

Oyster harvest played a minor role in predicting parasite patterns. Harvested reefs had lower habitat scores, as expected (Table S2), with higher densities and greater reef height on unharvested reefs consistent with management goals for these reefs (MVORIW 2022). Cliona sponge intensity was the only parasite metric that differed based on harvest status, with reduced sponge intensity on the harvested reefs relative to the unharvested reefs. This agrees with a prior study that detected lower intensities of Cliona sponge in oysters on harvested reefs compared to reefs in sanctuaries where harvest is prohibited. However, that study also found lower intensities of Polydora worm mud blisters in harvested areas that were not detected here (Anchondo et al. 2024). Across multiple species, the impact of fishing on parasite abundance has been shown to depend on features of the hosts and parasites. For example, a meta-analysis of 74 parasite pairs found no change in parasite abundance in fished species (Wood & Lafferty 2015). Wood et al. (2014) found higher parasite abundance on fished islands in the Line Islands Archipelago, but this was only true of directly transmitted parasites and not trophically transmitted parasites. The decrease in parasite intensity for Cliona shell parasites and not for Polydora worms or P. marinus on harvested reefs suggests there are other relevant mechanisms at play, as all three are waterborne parasites (Hopkins 1958, Pomponi & Meritt 1985, Chu 1996)

From a mechanistic standpoint, oysters on unharvested reefs may have higher Cliona sponge infection intensities if they are less able to defend against disease due to lower body condition, which has been associated with sanctuary oysters in Chesapeake Bay in previous work (Anchondo et al. 2024). Shell height and shell area did not differ between unharvested and harvested reefs in this study (Figure S2). The Harris Creek, Great Wicomico, and James River harvest/unharvested paired sites drive the relationship between harvest and Cliona sponge intensity (Figure 8). This reef-level spatial variation provides insight because these sites do not stand out as environmentally distinct: they are one lower-bay, one mid-bay and one upper-bay site and the paired sites are near each other (Figure 1). Additional factors not measured here that could explain the link between harvest and bio-eroding Cliona intensity are shell density, pH, and calcium carbonate concentrations. Shell density is a compelling direction for future study because the 3-dimensional growth structure of unharvested reefs can lead to different growth patterns that may influence the shell structure and palatability to Cliona boring sponges.

Harvest status was not linked to changes in prevalence for any of the three parasites, including Cliona sponge, suggesting harvest status does not affect transmission and that unharvested reefs may not be a source of parasites for harvested reefs. Notably, harvested and unharvested reefs did not differ in the prevalence or intensity of the especially damaging P. marinus infections. The role of harvest status in Cliona intensity but not Cliona prevalence is an unexpected mismatch that expands on previous theoretical scenarios for fishing impacts on disease (e.g. Wood et al. 2010) because few studies address both prevalence and intensity relationships with harvest. Intensity is theoretically positively related to prevalence for these parasites because higher-intensity infections mean more parasites per host, elevated parasite shedding, more transmission, and thus increased prevalence. Our findings are novel because Cliona sponge intensity is elevated on unharvested reefs with no accompanying increase in prevalence and no differences in size structure by harvest status (Figure S2). One possible explanation is that oysters on unharvested and harvested reefs are all equally exposed to sponge propagules, and thus have similar prevalence, but that oysters on unharvested reefs have lower resistance (perhaps due to lower body condition) and are unable to prevent parasites from reaching high intensities. This scenario warrants additional exploration.

Reef habitat characteristics reflected by habitat scores, i.e., oyster percent cover and reef height (Figure S1), rarely predicted prevalence or intensity for the three parasites. However, Polydora mud blister intensity was lower on reefs with higher oyster percent cover and high vertical relief, and P. marinus prevalence was lower in habitats with high oyster percent cover and low vertical relief. Neither Polydora intensity nor P. marinus prevalence simply increased with greater numbers of oysters, as occurs in previous studies of P. marinus prevalence (Hanley et al. 2019) and would be expected if transmission is density dependent. The decrease in Polydora mud blister intensity on reefs with higher oyster percent cover is consistent with oysters filtering out parasites (Ben-Horin et al. 2018), but the lowest P. marinus prevalence at intermediate habitat scores is not. The habitat score is a proxy for the oyster population and not a direct metric of live oysters. Specific measures of harvest pressure, such as number of bushels harvested from the reef, would better evaluate harvest as a predictor of parasite metrics, but these data are not currently available.

The spatial models provide key insights into multiple parasite patterns in Chesapeake Bay oysters because all three parasites varied with reef or tributary identity. Spatial models outperformed the multivariate models in explaining disease patterns for P. marinus, Cliona sponges, and Polydora worms. The low R2 values for the best multivariate model of Polydora intensity indicates low explanatory power, but it is improved for the best spatial model. This shows that unmeasured and spatially clustered conditions, host traits, or stochastic processes are important (Seabloom et al. 2015). The consistent importance of reef-level spatial variation highlights that sources of variation occur at relatively small spatial scales. The importance of reef-level spatial variation over tributary-level spatial variation for most parasites is unexpected because each of the Chesapeake Bay tributaries has unique conditions borne of hydrology, geology, and watershed characteristics, including upstream land use (Boynton et al. 1995). The role of the reef identity in spatial models may reflect the combined importance of tributary and salinity. Additional environmental conditions that could account for variation at the reef scale include dissolved oxygen concentrations; current patterns; bottom type, such as mud vs. sand; or other factors not included in this study. Restoration and enhancement plantings of oyster spat also typically occur at the reef scale, meaning that reef-level parasite variation could result from oyster genotypes with different disease resistance that cluster by reef. Hanley et al. (2023) recently identified strong links between oyster genetic identity and these same parasites in Rhode Island. They compared parasite communities in C. virginica oysters sourced from three genetic backgrounds, confirmed genetic differences with microsatellites, and detected different parasite communities in the three genetic groups for four years post-restoration (Hanley et al. 2023).

The salinity gradient and management strategies on oyster reefs in Chesapeake Bay create a natural experiment through which to advance knowledge on co-infection amidst natural and anthropogenic conditions. This large-scale survey of 16 paired reefs in 8 tributaries provides a snapshot of disease dynamics in Chesapeake Bay across approximately 200 km. It shows that understanding reef-level variation is a priority and that, despite high environmental and spatial variation, co-infection predicts parasite patterns. The role of co-infection in this correlational study merits further investigation in an experimental framework, not least because of the growing importance of reef-associated biodiversity as restoration efforts progress in Chesapeake Bay. Bio-eroding parasites are potential risk factors for the notorious dermo disease outbreaks caused by P. marinus. Likewise, P. marinus could increase damaging shell infestations by bio-eroders.

The role of co-infection and the multivariate drivers of disease highlight knowledge gaps for globally threatened oyster populations (Beck et al. 2011). Most oyster populations are impacted by microparasitic infections by bacteria, viruses, and/or protists (Davison et al. 2005, Burge et al. 2014, King et al. 2019), and shell-boring parasites are widespread and continuing to invade new populations (Martinelli et al. 2020, Tan et al. 2023). Integrating multiple natural and anthropogenic conditions into our understanding of disease in wild populations is necessary to move beyond canonical abiotic drivers and improve management strategies.
